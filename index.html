02 / The craft

The difference is
in the joinery.

The dovetail notch comes from an older way of building with logs. Each corner is cut to fit the timber around it, so the walls lock together instead of meeting at a standard connection. As the logs settle, the joint stays engaged. A dovetail notch locks one wall into the next, creating a tight corner that settles with the logs.

Most kit homes begin with milled logs cut to a common pattern. That makes construction faster, but the connection is determined by the kit rather than by the logs on site. A hand-cut dovetail takes more time and skill. It creates a direct, lasting connection between the walls and lets the house follow the timber instead of forcing the timber into a standard part.

05 / About us

Steve Braun
A master of his trade.

Steve Braun learned to scribe logs from his grandfather at age five. At seventeen, he built his first cabin in the Gallatin Range, cutting and fitting the logs by hand.

He still works that way today. Steve peels and cuts each log himself. He uses a hand-cut dovetail joint his grandfather taught him, along with saddle notches when a project calls for them.

Most log homes are built from milled kits or machine-cut parts. Steve keeps this older method alive. It takes more time, but it lets him fit the home to the logs instead of making the logs fit a kit.

He has built homes across the continental United States from coast to coast, as well as on remote Alaskan islands.
